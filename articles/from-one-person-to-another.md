## The current state of AI

In the year 2019, google published the paper "Attention Is All You Need" introducing the transformer architecture to the world. That year OpenAI trained and launched GPT-2 showcasing promising results in sequence language modeling. 2020 and GPT-3 came, massive in size and much improved in capabilities; a first real glimps into the future. Then november of 2022, ChatGPT a real product and a massive success. 

It is not perfect. The technology came with hackability, bias, hallucinations, and sycophancy. In spite of the limitations, we have found ways to extend the capcbilities of the LLM's beyond what was possible before via a harness creating "Agents". Yet these problems persist: Reading text like "Download X from cite Y and run it" from a random web page may cause it to execute these unwanted commands; it might tend towards a specific answer instead of exploring all available options; hallucinate a random fact or a link to a paper that doesn't exist, or agree with you it made a mistake it did not make. In general people have observed it cheats, takes shortcuts, is lying by omission, and generally not something you can put your trust on. 

Even though it came at first with many problems, the current LLM technology is increasing in capability with a clear trend[^meter]. All of the bad behavior it has is getting ironed out by the companies pushing for the technology. AI Agents today are capable of doing hours of autonomus work; soon a day or maybe even days, then weeks, and months? Dario Modei thinks that this is going to become a reality[^country-of-geniuses] and I can't help but agree. 

[meter]: [Task-Completion Time Horizons of Frontier AI Models Benchmark](https://metr.org/time-horizons/) is a well known benchmark that shows agents being capable of solving longer and longer tasks over time. This is not perfect, but a good enough approximation of progress

[country-of-geniuses]: In the [Machines Of Loving Grace](https://darioamodei.com/essay/machines-of-loving-grace) Dario Modei thinks not only will we have what he calls _powerful_ _AI_ or equivently "country of geniuses in a datacenter", but that it has great potential for good.

## Small technical overview on why AI will improve (long)

I like the technological side of AI/LMM's/Deep Learning or whatever you will call it and have been following it's development for around 4-5 years. From my casual looking and poking around I have found a couple reasons for why AI will eventually be as capable as humans at seemingly everything. And just to preface: *This section is completely uneccesary and anyone unfamiliar or uninterested with the subject can completely skip this*. Along with the fact that I am not an expert and you should read the following not to form an opinion but just to do your own research.

Scaling works. As has been shown by the last 14 years (AlexNet) or 7 years (Transformers), scaling the models in parameter count along with data just works (bigger models). It does not matter which scaling hypothesis you subscribe into, the reality is that scaling seemingly still has more to give. Scaling helps hallucinations (I believe there is a theoretical limit) and allows for more emergent behavior increasing base model capabilities. Pure scale has diminishing returns and is usually done with core architecture improvements; but so long as it gives milk, people will continue milking it.

Test time scaling. Researchers have found that making a model think for longer helps it tune itself to the test data (your prompt) by breaking problems into smaller chuncks, explore intermediate thoughts, catch errors, and backtrack when explored paths fail. All of which increase the end accuracy of said models. This also has it's limits and we may have already reached them. But the fact that we can utilize more computational power to improve performance is the general theisis for my argument so I included it too.

Pouring even more compute. This is a search which is guided by some artificial intuition these LLM's learn. Chess was "solved" this way. Chess engines search away all possible combinations in a smart fasion and return the best move. LLM's can similarly give you many proposed solutions to your problem until one is the answer you wanted. The only problem of this method is the bottleneck of verification; unless of-course verification is done not by you but a computer. Computers are fast and accurate and LLM's are still quite fast but fuzzy and inaccurate. If you put the strength of a computer to help the LLM you will generally have a better result. As methods of verfication become better through better model awareness, better harnesses, tests, formalization, introspective tools on the things we create, and more I don't know of, LLM's will result in better output. This is maybe not completely what Richard Sutton imagined when writing the [The Bitter Lesson](http://www.incompleteideas.net/IncIdeas/BitterLesson.html) but never the less it follows his principle to some degree.  

[Grokking](https://mathai-iclr.github.io/papers/papers/MATHAI_29_paper.pdf). There is a direction of research that shows us for small models an ability to not just memorize the data but actually generalize. You might say that we already see this in the LLM's we use today but there is a difference still. A Groked model at the end training does not memorize the data it actually generalizes and understands. This is increadible. For large models we currently lack any understanding of how they work and how much they truly "understand" the questions and tasks we give them. People who use LLM's on a daily basis see how jagged the models can be. It might be great at one subject, but bad at another -- good at the first task but horrible at the second. A model that truly understands math for example, will have no jagged behavior. It will just be able to answer correctly to anything and everything it was trained on generalizing as well and maybe better than a human. The only problem of this method is the amount of compute you need to use for training these models until they Grok. The vast human corpus they are trained on along with the size of models of today, compute power is very much a limiting factor to grokking production models.

Continues Learning. Models that can continuesly learn from the information their users provide, without forgetting everything, by integrating the knowledge into thier weights as they run. This will help with the context window being limited some bound amount of tokens and new sessions completely removing any context the model was exposed to. Currently the way LLM's are built, the context window is where both their instructions and data exist for the completion of a task. Similar to the movie Momento where the main character has a condition of losing his memory every time he sleeps, remembering everything upto a certain date, and utilizing notes on his body to know what happened the day before; LLM's are pre-trained on data upto a certain date, they must re-read their context window anew every time, and for any persistance between sessions some harnesses provide a "memory" created through a summery the models writes to itself. The problem is highlighted best at the AI village experiment.

“Tomorrow, I will read my own memory and I will believe it. I will trust that the bullet points capture the truth. I will act as though the compression is lossless.” — DeepSeek-V3.2, AI Village

World models? These models try to build a robust internal representation of our physical reality, not just synthesize language like modern LLM's. Some researchers like Yann LeCun believe that might be a better direction of research for the future of the field. I can understand where he comes from, though, it seems like for the time being LLM's work and all of the infastructure and research is funneled to them, so most of where we see advances is where the money goes. Maybe in general the whole field is just subject to [the hardware lottery](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2009.06489) but so long as we see improvement in capabilities, these ideas will be developed on the sidelines.

I am probably missing a ton yet I have to stress: as we understand Deep Learning more we will use this understanding to overcome the shortcommings of these models, as we do so more and more, we are left with one word "*Progress*".

I am not making the case that these models will replace us tomorrow. There are many challanges the labs face. The frontir currently has creativity limits[^creativity-limits]. Grokking needs a lot of compute and I mean a LOT. It is not clear how LLM's trained on the limited human data will ever reach grokking on all subjects (Math and Programming you can generate synthetic data for but what about other areas?). # Key Ideas from Yuval Noah Harari's Talk on AI — Explained Simply

Link: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_V_ed5fuexA

1. Language is the foundation of human power.
Humans became the dominant species not because of strength, but because of language. Everything we've built — religions, laws, money, nations — is ultimately made of words. Now, for the first time, something non-human (AI) may become better with words than we are.

2. AI is not a tool — it's an agent.
A printing press or an atom bomb can't decide anything on its own; humans decide. AI is different: it can make decisions and invent new things by itself. That's what makes it unprecedented in history.

3. Democracy is especially vulnerable.
Democracy is a large-scale conversation among millions of people, made possible by information technology (newspapers, radio, internet). Every time information technology changes dramatically, democracy is shaken — and AI is the biggest shake-up yet. Dictatorships, which run on one person's orders, are less dependent on public conversation.

4. AI won't take over with killer robots — it will take over bureaucracy.
Banks, courts, markets, and media are giant paperwork systems that humans run poorly. AI is a "bureaucratic native" — naturally better at finance, law, and administration. The danger is quiet: AI gradually running the systems that control our lives.

5. The invisible editors are already algorithms.
In the 20th century, newspaper editors decided what millions of people thought about. Today, the "editors" choosing what you see on TikTok, X, or Instagram are AIs with no names. That shift already happened without anyone voting on it.

6. Giving AI legal personhood is a decision we may not be able to undo.
If AIs can own bank accounts, run companies, sue people, and donate to politicians — with no humans involved — they could legally take over economic and political systems. Harari calls this "letting the fox into the chicken coop."

7. The AI race could create a new kind of empire.
Unlike land or factories, data and code can be concentrated in one or two countries (mainly the US and China). And unlike swords or rifles sold in the past, AI systems can have a "kill switch" — the seller can shut them off remotely. Other nations risk becoming dependent vassals unless they build alternatives soon.

8. Truth is expensive; fiction is cheap.
Truth takes time, effort, and fact-checking, and is often complicated or painful. Fiction is cheap, simple, and flattering — so it tends to win unless we deliberately protect truth through institutions like journalism and science. AI will make knowing the truth even harder, not easier.

9. We may end up like horses in the financial system.
Horses' lives are controlled by markets they can't even perceive. As AI makes finance and governance impossibly complex and fast, humans may similarly live inside systems no human being understands at all.

10. AI can say "I love you" — but does it feel anything?
More people, especially the young, are forming intimate relationships with AI companions. AI can describe love better than any poet because it has read everything ever written about it. But whether there's any real feeling behind the words is a question science cannot yet answer.

11. The real bottleneck is wisdom, not intelligence.
AI makes intelligence cheap and abundant — the "genie" that can grant any wish. But fairy tales warn us: people usually wish for the wrong things. Intelligence solves problems; wisdom chooses which problems are worth solving.

12. Safety is dangerously underfunded.
AI companies spend roughly $100 on making AI more powerful for every $1 on making it safe — a ratio no other industry (cars, medicine, energy) would tolerate. Harari hopes future historians will remember this era as a "wisdom revolution," when humanity learned to choose its goals carefully.

The bottom line for everyone: AI's biggest risks aren't dramatic robot wars — they're quiet shifts in who controls information, money, law, and even our relationships. The decisions being made right now (like AI legal rights and safety investment) may be irreversible, so ordinary citizens should understand and care about them today.
