## Never ending problems ( why is it hard to kill all utility jobs )

If we look at it from first-principles, jobs in the economy are trying to solve problems for people. This problem-solving engine may help byeond just human physical necessities. 

I believe so long as AI is not _all_ _powerful_ we will have more net jobs. People constantly reference [Jevons paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jevons_paradox) -- if you make something more efficient, we end up consuming more of it -- not less. The paradox applies to resources, so AI usage will increase if the cost of AI decreases, something we do observe; but how would that effect jobs? If whole point of AI is to automate the automation -- cheep intellegence for everyone -- how then can it increase jobs?  Technological revolutions I argue, bring with them a new set of problems to solve thus, more jobs -- AI will be no different. 

Some 10 thousand years ago, when hunter gatherers transitioned into a farming based society, growing a small number of crops (wheat, corn, rice, potatoes, millet) and allowing for the creation of a food surplus; unknowingly their decision created more problems to solve. Ancient farmers had to work the land removing rocks and pebbles, watching out for any worm or bug that might infect the plant, or hungry animals waiting to eat their precious food. Watering is important along with fertilizer for land constantly sucked out of minerals. So much time was spent cultivating wheat, settlements formed around farmland, anchoring humans in place. So much for solving a problem.

Society continued, the masses spending much of their time working the land, as the population steadily increased. More population? more problems! Social problems of governence for the small independent and largely self-sufficient communities with population boom. Practical problems of farmers, medicinal, and other areas of life that while being a hunter gather you did not have time to do. We continued on this tranjectory until the invention of the machine. 

The industrial revolution brought with it machines that gave us massive automation to many areas of life; the most impactful of which was farming. The rise of machines may have caused farming to now become a small area of our life, and yet new problems greet us. Building, maintaining, and utilizing machines to create more goods. With hunger becoming a less significant part of peoples lives, and creation of comodities cheepens, more problems arise. The person creating machines needs tools to help him, the factory worker also needs tools to make his job fast, along with the wide varaiety of maintenence jobs created around a growing industry. Rural areas abandoned to search for opportunities in the city, and now you need a way to support so many people in a small space. Housing is reworked, transportation too, all areas of life are impacted. And the trend continues.

The invention of the computer helped machines opearte in more complex ways than ever before. And to not belabor the point any longer, it made more problems now dealing with information processing, and the way which we all overcome it was the solution. From that came the airplane and many more ways in which transportation housing and so much was once again changed. # Key Ideas from Yuval Noah Harari's Talk on AI — Explained Simply

Link: https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_V_ed5fuexA

1. Language is the foundation of human power.
Humans became the dominant species not because of strength, but because of language. Everything we've built — religions, laws, money, nations — is ultimately made of words. Now, for the first time, something non-human (AI) may become better with words than we are.

2. AI is not a tool — it's an agent.
A printing press or an atom bomb can't decide anything on its own; humans decide. AI is different: it can make decisions and invent new things by itself. That's what makes it unprecedented in history.

3. Democracy is especially vulnerable.
Democracy is a large-scale conversation among millions of people, made possible by information technology (newspapers, radio, internet). Every time information technology changes dramatically, democracy is shaken — and AI is the biggest shake-up yet. Dictatorships, which run on one person's orders, are less dependent on public conversation.

4. AI won't take over with killer robots — it will take over bureaucracy.
Banks, courts, markets, and media are giant paperwork systems that humans run poorly. AI is a "bureaucratic native" — naturally better at finance, law, and administration. The danger is quiet: AI gradually running the systems that control our lives.

5. The invisible editors are already algorithms.
In the 20th century, newspaper editors decided what millions of people thought about. Today, the "editors" choosing what you see on TikTok, X, or Instagram are AIs with no names. That shift already happened without anyone voting on it.

6. Giving AI legal personhood is a decision we may not be able to undo.
If AIs can own bank accounts, run companies, sue people, and donate to politicians — with no humans involved — they could legally take over economic and political systems. Harari calls this "letting the fox into the chicken coop."

7. The AI race could create a new kind of empire.
Unlike land or factories, data and code can be concentrated in one or two countries (mainly the US and China). And unlike swords or rifles sold in the past, AI systems can have a "kill switch" — the seller can shut them off remotely. Other nations risk becoming dependent vassals unless they build alternatives soon.

8. Truth is expensive; fiction is cheap.
Truth takes time, effort, and fact-checking, and is often complicated or painful. Fiction is cheap, simple, and flattering — so it tends to win unless we deliberately protect truth through institutions like journalism and science. AI will make knowing the truth even harder, not easier.

9. We may end up like horses in the financial system.
Horses' lives are controlled by markets they can't even perceive. As AI makes finance and governance impossibly complex and fast, humans may similarly live inside systems no human being understands at all.

10. AI can say "I love you" — but does it feel anything?
More people, especially the young, are forming intimate relationships with AI companions. AI can describe love better than any poet because it has read everything ever written about it. But whether there's any real feeling behind the words is a question science cannot yet answer.

11. The real bottleneck is wisdom, not intelligence.
AI makes intelligence cheap and abundant — the "genie" that can grant any wish. But fairy tales warn us: people usually wish for the wrong things. Intelligence solves problems; wisdom chooses which problems are worth solving.

12. Safety is dangerously underfunded.
AI companies spend roughly $100 on making AI more powerful for every $1 on making it safe — a ratio no other industry (cars, medicine, energy) would tolerate. Harari hopes future historians will remember this era as a "wisdom revolution," when humanity learned to choose its goals carefully.

The bottom line for everyone: AI's biggest risks aren't dramatic robot wars — they're quiet shifts in who controls information, money, law, and even our relationships. The decisions being made right now (like AI legal rights and safety investment) may be irreversible, so ordinary citizens should understand and care about them today.
